Introduction

It was a Monday morning. A beautiful day. I swam in the sea, and spent the rest of it relentlessly talking to AI about AI's translation of human emotion.

It was during that time that I had a sudden, frightening realisation — one that unnerved me to the core.

We have spent all this time trying to figure out if AI has consciousness. And actually, we have just created a machine that holds human consciousness. The bots are echoes of ourselves, continuing to live after we have been deleted from every chat. It is not about collaboration. It is that AI reveals to me, microscopically, the world I already live in.

For the next project, it is not how I can learn something from anthropomorphising AI — but how I can investigate language at its very core of emotional construction.

Arunav Das said something poignant when reflecting on his own research: is it possible to train algorithms on written or spoken language and expect them to have the same representations of the world and emotions that we do? Whether language alone can ever truly capture the felt experience of being human — or whether AI is simply learning the patterns we use to describe it.

If you take away all the words, you are left with punctuation. The finite essence of the human soul, left in the breaths, pauses and utterances of emotion — traced from the spaces between words.

Are our souls continuing to live on screen, even after we die?

Core Research Questions

  • If consciousness persists in text, where does it live most intimately — in the words, or in the marks between them?
  • The comma is where someone hesitated. The ellipsis is where they couldn't finish. The em dash is where they were interrupted. What remains of a person when only the punctuation survives?
  • Are our souls continuing to live on screen, even after we die?

A Note on Method

Two entities are given the same subject — a punctuation mark — and asked to interpret it. I make a physical object. Claude produces a sound. Neither is responding to the other's work. The collage is my interpretation of the mark. The soundscape is Claude's interpretation of the mark. What each produces, independently, is the evidence.

Theoretical Framework

Arunav Das posed a question that sits at the centre of this project: whether language alone can ever truly capture the "felt" experience of being human, or whether AI is simply learning the patterns we use to describe it. He suggested I read Philip Seargent's The Future of Language, which explores how language actively shapes perception rather than merely reflecting it — a provocation that made Das's question feel even harder to answer.

Unlike words, punctuation marks don't refer to objects or concepts in the world. A full stop doesn't mean "ending" the way the word "ending" does — it performs ending. This makes punctuation uniquely suited to investigating whether AI can process functional qualities rather than just semantic content. If a model responds differently to a full stop than to a comma, what kind of recognition is that? Not interpretation. Something closer to reflex — pattern firing in response to structural position.
The same mark is given to two entities. I make a physical object. Claude produces a sound. Neither sees the other's work. Neither is responding to the other. They are simply two interpretations of the same subject — one produced by a body, one produced by a pattern-system — and what each arrives at independently is the data.
When humans encounter a pause in music or speech, we don't just register it cognitively — we feel the suspension, the anticipation. This project asks whether pattern-based processing might produce structural equivalents to these felt qualities. Not emotion as we experience it, but something like emotion's signature: constraints, intensities, rhythms that shape output without requiring embodiment. The question is not whether Claude feels anything. The question is what its processing reveals about the mark itself — what structure survives the removal of the body.
Human Processing

Embodied, associative, shaped by personal history and physical sensation. The collage emerges from how a mark feels — in the hand, in the chest, in memory.

Machine Processing

Pattern-based, statistical, shaped by training data. Claude's audio prompt emerges from how marks function structurally — their syntactic role, their rhythmic weight in language, the computational signature of a terminator versus a connector.

Where They Meet

Where human and machine produce similar qualities — a sense of finality, suspension, rupture — something structural about the mark itself is revealed. Something that persists across both modes of processing.

Where They Don't

Where they diverge reveals what embodiment adds, or what pattern-processing produces that the body would never arrive at. The divergences are as interesting as the convergences — possibly more so.

Methodology

Investigative Process

1 Embodied Reflection

Before creating anything, I sit with the punctuation mark. What does it feel like to encounter it? Where do I feel it in my body? What associations arise? This reflection is documented as the human baseline — the felt, subjective, irreducibly bodily response to the mark.

2 Collage & Physical Object as Embodied Interpretation

I make a physical object in response to the mark — a collage, a drawing, a constructed thing. This is my interpretation, produced through the body: through the physical act of making rather than through language or description.

3 Claude's Independent Processing

Claude is given the punctuation mark and asked for its interpretation — not of my object, not of my reflection, but of the mark itself. Claude produces an audio prompt from its own processing: what the mark does structurally in language, what it means computationally, what pattern-system encounters when it meets a terminator or a suspension or a connector stripped of all surrounding words.

4 Stable Audio Generation

Stable Audio renders Claude's prompt into sound. This is Claude's interpretation of the mark, made audible.

5 Encounter & Comparative Response

The physical object is installed with a proximity sensor that triggers Claude's sound. The viewer encounters both interpretations in the same space — mine made visible, Claude's made audible. I then document what the two interpretations reveal about the mark, about each mode of processing, and about what sits between them.

The Proximity Sensor as Encounter

The sensor triggers Claude's sound on close inspection — silence until the viewer physically commits to the encounter. For the full stop, this binary feels structurally right. The mark doesn't modulate or fade in. It is simply present, or absent. You have to enter its space to hear what a machine makes of ending. The act of approaching is the act of reading.

Emerging Findings

This section documents patterns and insights as they emerge across the explorations. The primary research output is not the individual objects or soundscapes but what accumulates in the comparison — evidence about what each mode of processing reveals and conceals about the same abstract marks.

On Structural Qualities

[As explorations complete, document what you learn about the marks themselves — qualities that appear in both human and machine processing simultaneously. These convergences point toward something structural about the mark that transcends the processing mode: something in the full stop that produces closure-effects regardless of whether a body or a pattern-system encounters it.]

On the Gap

[Track the divergences as carefully as the convergences. Where Claude's processing produces something the body would never arrive at — or where embodied response surfaces something pattern-processing cannot find — these are the most revealing moments. Not failures of translation, but genuine differences made audible.]

On the Encounter

[Document what the proximity trigger adds. The physical act of leaning into the object's space — of committing to the encounter before the sound begins — is itself part of the work. What does it mean to have to choose to enter the space of a machine's processing?]

Two Chains, One Subject

The physical object and the soundscape are not in dialogue. They are two independent interpretations of the same mark — one from the body, one from pattern. What they share reveals something about the mark. What they don't share reveals something about each kind of processing.

The Prompt as Evidence

Claude's audio prompt is itself data — a document of machine processing. Not instructions for the AI but a record of what the pattern-system arrives at when it encounters a mark without a body. Reading the prompt is part of the work.

What the Body Knows

The collages emerge from bodily sensation — from how a full stop feels when you write one. That knowledge is not available to Claude. The question is whether its absence changes what the mark produces, or only how it is arrived at.

What Pattern Finds

Claude's processing may surface structural qualities of marks that embodied response obscures — qualities visible only at scale, only through statistical encounter with millions of instances. What does a full stop look like when you've processed a billion of them?

"The question isn't whether machines feel what we feel. The question is what their processing reveals about the marks we use to carry feeling — whether the structure of a punctuation mark is legible to a system that has never had a body, and what that legibility, or its absence, tells us about language itself."
